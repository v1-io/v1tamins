# SUCCESs Framework -- Extended Reference

Deep-dive examples and techniques for each principle, drawn from *Made to Stick* by Chip Heath & Dan Heath.

## 1. SIMPLE -- Find the Core

### Key Concept: Commander's Intent (CI)

The U.S. Army's solution to the problem that "no plan survives contact with the enemy." CI is a crisp, plain-talk statement at the top of every order specifying the desired end-state. It never specifies so much detail that it risks being rendered obsolete by unpredictable events.

**Application:** Every message needs a Commander's Intent. Ask:
- "If we do nothing else during tomorrow's mission, we must ___"
- "The single, most important thing we must do tomorrow is ___"

### Key Example: Southwest Airlines

Herb Kelleher's CI: "We are THE low-fare airline." This one idea guided thousands of employees across decades. When marketing suggested adding chicken Caesar salad to flights: "Will adding that salad make us THE low-fare airline? No? Then we're not serving any damn chicken salad."

### Key Example: Clinton 1992

James Carville wrote three phrases on a whiteboard. "It's the economy, stupid" became the core. His rule: "If you say three things, you don't say anything." Forced prioritization.

### Key Example: The Dunn Daily Record

Newspaper with 112% community penetration. Publisher's CI: "Names, names, and names." Simple, concrete, actionable. Every staffer could apply it to every decision.

### Technique: Simple = Core + Compact

- Core: the essential insight (forced prioritization to find it)
- Compact: expressed concisely using existing schemas
- Proverbs are the ideal: simple AND profound
- NOT dumbing down. NOT sound bites. It's about elegance and prioritization.

### Technique: Generative Analogies

Use what the audience already knows. Tap existing schemas:
- "A pomelo is basically a supersized grapefruit" (schema + modifier)
- "Jaws on a spaceship" = Alien (high concept = schema + twist)
- Disney calls employees "cast members" -- this single analogy changes everything about how they think about their roles

## 2. UNEXPECTED -- Get and Keep Attention

### Getting Attention: Break the Schema

The "surprise brow" -- an involuntary response that focuses attention and increases memory. But surprise must be followed by insight ("postdictability").

**Gimmicky surprise** = shock without insight (jumping out from behind a door)
**Sticky surprise** = schema violation that reveals a core truth

### Key Example: Nordstrom Stories

New employees hear: A Nordie gift-wrapped a Macy's purchase. A Nordie refunded money for tire chains -- even though Nordstrom doesn't sell tire chains. These stories break the schema of "good customer service" and replace it with something uncommon.

### Key Example: Nora Ephron's Journalism Teacher

Students expected journalism = who, what, when, where, why. The teacher gave them facts and they wrote boring leads. Then he revealed: "The lead to the story is 'There will be no school next Thursday.'" This schema-breaking moment stuck with Ephron for 30+ years.

### Keeping Attention: Gap Theory of Curiosity

George Loewenstein (1994): Curiosity happens when we feel a gap in our knowledge. Gaps cause pain -- like an itch we need to scratch.

**Techniques for opening gaps:**
1. Pose a question or puzzle
2. Point out that someone else knows something they don't
3. Present situations with unknown resolutions
4. Challenge them to predict an outcome (creates two gaps: "What will happen?" and "Was I right?")

**Critical insight:** You must open gaps BEFORE you close them. Our tendency is to give answers without first making people care about the questions.

### Key Example: Saturn's Rings

An astronomer opened with: "How can we account for the rings of Saturn?" Then: "How could three internationally acclaimed groups of scientists come to wholly different conclusions?" This mystery structure made dust interesting for 20 pages.

### Key Example: Roone Arledge and NCAA Football

How do you make viewers care about a Michigan vs. Ohio State game they have no stake in? Arledge's answer: provide context. Show the campus, the fans, the rivalries. Fill in enough knowledge so that gaps (Who will win? What will happen?) become compelling.

## 3. CONCRETE -- Make It Real

### Key Concept: The Velcro Theory of Memory

Memory is like Velcro -- the more hooks an idea has, the better it sticks. Concrete details provide hooks. Abstract ideas are smooth and slippery.

### Key Example: The Leather Computer

Jerry Kaplan pitched to Kleiner Perkins with no slides, no business plan -- just a maroon leather portfolio. He tossed it on the table: "Here is a model of the next step in the computer revolution." The VCs couldn't stop touching it. They invested $4.5 million in a company that didn't exist yet.

### Key Example: White Things

"List as many white things as you can think of" vs. "List as many white things in your refrigerator as you can think of." People list nearly as many items for the refrigerator -- because concreteness mobilizes the brain.

### Key Example: Oral Rehydration Therapy

UNICEF director James Grant carried a packet of salt and sugar everywhere. "Do you know that this costs less than a cup of tea and it can save hundreds of thousands of children's lives in your country?" A prop beats a scientific description. It brings people to the table.

### Key Example: Saddleback Sam

A church with 50,000 members created a detailed persona: "Saddleback Sam is the typical unchurched man who lives in our area. Late thirties or early forties..." Everyone in the organization knows who Sam is and can make decisions through his lens.

### Key Example: The Ferraris Go to Disney World

HP needed to pitch technology to Disney executives. Stone Yamashita built a 6,000 sq ft exhibit following a fictional family (the Ferraris) through their Disney vacation. Abstract e-services became a living, breathing simulation.

### Technique: Make Decisions Through Specific People

Hamburger Helper brand manager visited customers' homes instead of reading "death binders" of data. "I'll never forget one woman who had a toddler on her hip while mixing up dinner on the stove." This concrete experience drove better decisions than any spreadsheet.

## 4. CREDIBLE -- Make It Believable

### External Credibility

**Authorities:** Experts, celebrities, aspirational figures
**Anti-authorities:** Real people with lived experience (often MORE credible than experts)
- Pam Laffin: not a doctor, just a 29-year-old smoker dying of emphysema. More compelling than any Surgeon General warning.
- Dennis the driver: formerly homeless, now driving grant evaluators. "He was living proof."

### Internal Credibility

**Vivid details:** Concrete specifics boost believability even when logically irrelevant
- "He uses a Star Wars toothbrush that looks like Darth Vader" -- made jurors rate the mother as more fit (5.8 vs 4.3 on a 10-point scale)
- Urban legends always include specific, localized details

**Human-scale statistics:**
- "Nuclear warheads as BBs" -- shrink the numbers to a physical demonstration
- "One soft drink per month would double our aid to Africa" -- human-scale comparison
- The Sinatra Test: "If I can make it there, I'll make it anywhere" -- one powerful example that proves everything

**Testable credentials:**
- Reagan: "Before you vote, ask yourself if you're better off than four years ago"
- Let the audience test the claim themselves instead of asking them to trust you

### Key Example: The Dancing Seventy-Three-Year-Old

Instead of quoting statistics about a fitness product, show the seventy-three-year-old dancing. The demonstration IS the proof.

## 5. EMOTIONAL -- Make Them Care

### The Mother Teresa Principle

"If I look at the mass, I will never act. If I look at the one, I will act." People are more likely to donate to a single identified child than to statistical summaries of millions suffering. Emotions don't scale.

### WIIFY: What's In It For You

Every local TV news teaser uses this: "There may be a new drug sweeping the community -- and it may be in your medicine cabinet!" Self-interest is the most reliable emotional trigger.

But don't stop at base self-interest. Move up Maslow's hierarchy:
- Cable TV ad targeting Texans: Don't just sell channels. Sell the ability to learn, to grow, to give your kids an edge. "Are you the kind of person who takes advantage of every opportunity?"

### Appeal to Identity

"Don't mess with Texas" -- the most successful anti-littering campaign in history. It worked because it appealed to Texan identity and pride, not guilt or statistics.

Ask: "What kind of person does my audience want to be? How does my message connect to that identity?"

### Avoid Semantic Stretch

Words like "unique," "quality," "excellence" have been stretched to meaninglessness. Find fresh emotional territory instead of relying on worn-out abstract terms.

## 6. STORIES -- Drive Action

### Three Universal Plot Types

**Challenge Plot:** Protagonist overcomes obstacles against the odds
- Jared the Subway dieter (425 lbs to 180 lbs eating Subway)
- David vs. Goliath
- Inspires people to take on challenges, work harder, take risks

**Connection Plot:** A relationship bridges a gap (racial, class, demographic)
- The Good Samaritan
- Inspires people to help others, be tolerant, work together

**Creativity Plot:** Someone makes a mental breakthrough, sees the world in a new way
- The apple falling on Newton
- Inspires people to experiment, think differently, innovate

### Stories as Flight Simulators

Research shows mentally rehearsing a situation helps us perform better when we encounter it. Firefighters swap stories after every fire -- building a mental catalog of scenarios and responses. Stories provide both:
- **Simulation:** knowledge about how to act
- **Inspiration:** motivation to act

### Springboard Stories

A single concrete example that helps people see the larger pattern. Stephen Denning at the World Bank used a story about a health worker in Zambia to springboard people's thinking about how knowledge management could transform the organization.

### Spotting vs. Creating

The best sticky stories aren't manufactured -- they're spotted. The Subway franchise owner who noticed Jared's transformation didn't create the story; he recognized its power and amplified it.

## The Villain: Curse of Knowledge

The tappers/listeners experiment (Elizabeth Newton, Stanford, 1990):
- Tappers tap the rhythm of well-known songs
- Listeners try to guess the song
- Tappers predict 50% success rate
- Actual success rate: 2.5% (3 out of 120)

The tapper hears the song in their head. The listener hears bizarre Morse code. Once you know something, you can't un-know it. Your knowledge has "cursed" you.

**Two ways to beat the Curse of Knowledge:**
1. Don't learn anything (not helpful)
2. Take your ideas and transform them using the SUCCESs checklist

The SUCCESs framework is the antidote to the Curse of Knowledge.
