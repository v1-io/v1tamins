# Visual recipe: illustrated everyday system

Prefer this recipe for queues, handoffs, policies, and processes whose behavior can be shown through one concrete example. Its identity comes from expressive drawing, deliberate timing, and understated humor. Choose a fresh setting and objects from the actual subject; a workshop is one possible setting, not a recurring requirement. Honor a requested style. Use a literal interface when interaction details are the lesson, or precise geometry when quantities and mathematical relationships are the lesson. No renderer, image generator, external assets, or speech provider is required, but the result must look like polished motion graphics rather than a slide deck.

## Appearance

- Start with warm paper, charcoal ink, and restrained mustard, teal, and coral accents. Suggested palette: paper `#FFF7E8`, ink `#393B3C`, mustard `#D8AD48`, teal `#327B7D`, coral `#CF725A`. Adapt it to the subject and readable contrast; limit any one scene to the accents it needs.
- Give every scene at least one drawn object a viewer would recognize without its label: a monitor, an envelope, a folder, a building, a person, a magnifying glass. A labelled rectangle is not an object. Add depth with layering, soft shadows, and nested views where they help.
- When a configured image generator is available, generated illustrations can supply scenery and props in the same palette and line style. Keep exact labels, numbers, and captions in the renderer, and label hypothetical people and data as examples.
- Draw recognizable objects with slightly uneven outlines, modest asymmetry, and optional subtle paper grain. Keep labels crisp. Avoid heavy texture and distressed text that interferes with reading.
- Follow an object relevant to the lesson. A small expression or distinctive posture can give it character when that helps the explanation. Preserve its silhouette, proportions, and color within the video. Do not require a recurring mascot or imply that a real automated system has judgment or feelings.
- Choose a coherent visual setting and only props that explain an action. Derive metaphors from the subject rather than repeating a stock scene or adding random gags.
- Use readable sans-serif labels and captions, with an optional restrained serif heading. Give one action most of the frame, leave generous empty space, and reserve caption space. Support color meanings with labels or shapes.

## Motion and voice

Move objects along eased paths so the viewer can follow them from one state to the next. Use camera push-ins to reveal detail, pull-outs to show context, and transitions that carry an object into the next scene. Give consequential actions a brief anticipation and reaction. Keep gentle motion on screen most of the time, with short holds after each new idea so the viewer can read it. Avoid constant bouncing, decorative spinning, and simulated drawing hands. Preserve object identity throughout.

Use warm, direct, deadpan narration. Explain the visible mechanism before adding a joke. Aim for at most one small gag or cheeky line per short explanation, chosen for that subject. Keep captions and technical terms accurate and straightforward.

Humor should illuminate the idea rather than mock users, affected people, or the seriousness of harm. Reduce or omit it for sensitive subjects and formal audiences. Never let a joke remove uncertainty or imply a result that the evidence does not support.

## Apply and check

Before full production, render a representative frame and a short motion beat with the chosen palette, type, objects, and caption position. Check legibility, continuity, the metaphor's causal meaning, and whether the humor helps the explanation. This is a production check, not another user approval gate.

Reject a metaphor if it needs more explanation than the actual process. Avoid sprawling whiteboards, stock office characters, status-card dashboards, boxes joined by lines, decorative slogans, and scenery competing with the action. Keep the drawing, timing, and voice coherent while letting each subject determine its own visual world.
