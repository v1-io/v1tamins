# Storyboard: make the explanation visible

## Compact working template

Question: What is the viewer trying to understand?
Prior knowledge: What can the explanation safely assume?
Takeaway: What should the viewer be able to explain afterward?

Visual direction: choose a setting, palette, type treatment, and consistent objects suited to this subject. Identify where captions will sit. Timing below is provisional until narration is generated.

| Beat | Viewer sees | Visible change | What it explains | Short narration | Cue / inspection pause |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opening | A concrete example of the problem | The problem occurs | Why the question matters | One or two spoken sentences | Event that starts the action |
| Mechanism | The same example, with needed detail | Construct, compare, move, or transform | How or why it works | Explain the visible action | Spoken event, then time to look |
| Payoff | The result beside its relevant earlier state | The consequence becomes visible | Answer to the opening | State the takeaway and material limit | Hold the result |

Add rows only when each has a distinct explanatory job. Record source support for consequential claims and what belongs in supporting notes. Explicitly label hypothetical examples. A real unresolved case must remain unresolved on screen.

## Weak and strong examples

### Software repair

Weak: seven count cards, ticket IDs, two pull-request numbers, status labels, and arrows; narration reads the audit summary.

Stronger: follow one illustrated repair. A broken connection is identified; a proposed patch bridges it; replay the same failing operation to check the result. Keep the repair recognizable throughout. Label the stages “Problem,” “Proposed fix,” and “Check.” A checkmark appears only after the illustrated check succeeds, and is scoped to that check. State that this is an example, not a claim that a live repair passed.

Possible narration: “A proposed fix is a candidate. We still need to check the problem that started this repair.” Keep the complete inventory and identifiers in the source notes.

### Mathematics: one half plus one third

Weak: display the fraction rule and read each algebraic step.

Stronger: show two equal-sized bars. Shade one half and one third; subdivide both into sixths without changing their total length. Move the three and two shaded pieces into a third equal-sized bar to show five sixths. Explain why the pieces need the same size before naming the common denominator. Preserve area during the transformation.

### Everyday process: finding a watering problem

Weak: a slide lists “observe, diagnose, act, monitor.”

Stronger: an explicitly hypothetical plant has dry soil. Show a moisture check, measured watering, then a later observation on a visible time change. Water arriving is an action; the later observation supplies evidence of its effect. Avoid implying that every drooping plant needs water or that recovery is immediate.

## Keep continuity and space

- Reuse an object's shape and color while it represents the same thing. Change those deliberately when its state changes.
- Use a wide view for orientation, then reveal or focus the relevant detail. Keep spatial relationships stable when they help the explanation.
- Let narration carry sentences; let the picture carry relationships and changes. Captions have their own reserved space.
- Use counts, terms, and exceptions only when removing them would change the lesson. Simplification must preserve uncertainty and causality.
