# Review the explanation and the finished video

## First-viewer pass: before final production

Give an independent reviewer only rough frames in order and the matching narration first. Ask for their inferred takeaway and answers below before sharing the brief and evidence. One focused reviewer is enough; use an available independent reviewer when authorized and justified. If reviewing your own work, state that it is a self-review with prior context.

First, look at each beat's frames with the narration hidden. If the viewer cannot say what changed and why, the narration is carrying the explanation; redesign the beat before reviewing anything else.

For each beat:

- What is happening, and what does the visible change mean?
- Why is this part here? How does it connect to the opening and previous beat?
- Which term, label, number, or jump requires knowledge the viewer has not been given?
- What mistaken conclusion could the picture imply?
- Is the beat only text, boxes, and lines? Where would a viewer's attention drop?
- Which ID, version, file name, or count would someone outside the work need to look up? Replace it with a plain name or move it to the notes.

Then compare the inferred takeaway with the intended lesson and check consequential claims against sources. Name each issue by beat, explain its effect, and give a concrete correction. Revise the rough sequence until identified material ambiguities are resolved; rereview changed beats and their connections. Record unresolved concerns rather than inventing a quality score.

Watch especially for action presented as outcome, a test presented as complete proof, a correlation presented as a cause, a hypothetical example presented as an observed result, or a compressed timeline that changes meaning. Preserve caveats that affect the conclusion in the video.

## Finished-video pass

Inspect the encoded artifact, not just the source renderer. When a parent agent delivers a child agent's video, the parent inspects the encoded frames itself; a production receipt is evidence, not approval.

- **Picture:** inspect the busiest frame at normal viewing size, each scene's representative frame, and transitions. Labels and captions must be legible, unclipped, and distinct. Check short-lived overlaps that a contact sheet can miss: inspect a short sequence of adjacent frames around suspect transitions, then replay the interval.
- **Motion:** changes must follow the explanation; preserve object identity, units, quantities, and causal order. Leave inspection time after a new visual idea.
- **Sound and timing:** play with sound when the host can. Check actual spoken words, pronunciation, natural pace, cue alignment, pauses, and caption accuracy. Stream presence, playback commands, or transcription alone do not prove listening quality.
- **Engagement:** each scene shows a recognizable object, moves smoothly without jitter or jagged edges, and keeps something happening between holds. A run of static labelled boxes fails this check.
- **Personality:** check that expressive motion or humor clarifies the lesson, suits the audience, and does not mock affected people. Remove competing scenery and decorative slogans. Check that visual jokes do not imply unsupported outcomes, including in paused frames.
- **Meaning:** confirm that the ending answers the opening and that editing has not removed a qualification or changed what an arrow, scale, or checkmark claims.
- **File:** inspect streams and duration with an available media inspector; verify playable video, captions, script, editable source, and source-note links.

Keep a compact review record in two separate parts. **Communication:** for each beat, whether its encoded frames show the intended change, plus issues and fixes. **Technical:** streams, duration, decode, captions, and rebuild. Never present the technical part as evidence of explanatory quality. Then list limits. If sound cannot be perceived or the user waived listening, say so and distinguish any transcription or playback checks performed; this is a disclosed limit, not a delivery gate or a new human approval step. A first-viewer agent pass can find problems; only actual viewer feedback can establish whether that viewer understood.
