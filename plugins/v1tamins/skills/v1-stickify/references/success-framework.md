# SUCCESs Framework

Examples from *Made to Stick* (Chip Heath & Dan Heath). Use the move.

## 1. SIMPLE -- Find the Core

**Commander's Intent.** One plain sentence for the end-state: "If we do nothing else, we must ___." Enough that people can improvise when the plan dies; not so much that a surprise makes it obsolete.

Southwest's was "We are THE low-fare airline." When marketing wanted chicken Caesar salad on flights: will that make us THE low-fare airline? No? Then we're not serving any damn chicken salad.

Carville's whiteboard: "It's the economy, stupid." His rule: if you say three things, you don't say anything.

**Simple = core + compact.** Forced priority, then a short form that uses a schema they already have. A proverb, not a mission statement. Not dumbing down.

**Tap a schema they know:**
- "A pomelo is a supersized grapefruit"
- "Jaws on a spaceship" = Alien
- Disney calls employees "cast members" — that one analogy changes how they act

## 2. UNEXPECTED -- Get and Keep Attention

Break what they expected, then leave a gap they want closed. Surprise only sticks if it makes sense looking back (postdictable). Jumping out from behind a door is a gimmick. A schema break that reveals the core is sticky.

Nordstrom onboarding: a Nordie gift-wrapped a Macy's purchase; another refunded tire chains Nordstrom doesn't sell. That breaks "good customer service" and replaces it with something uncommon.

Nora Ephron's journalism teacher had students write the who/what/when/where/why lead, then told them the lead was "There will be no school next Thursday." She remembered it for 30 years.

**Open the gap before you close it.** People ignore answers they didn't want yet.
1. Pose a question or puzzle
2. Point out that someone else knows something they don't
3. Leave a situation unresolved
4. Ask them to predict (two gaps: what happens, and was I right?)

Mystery holds attention: "How can we account for the rings of Saturn?" then "How could three acclaimed groups reach wholly different conclusions?" made dust interesting for 20 pages.

If they have no stake, fill in just enough context that the gaps matter — campus, fans, rivalry — then "who wins?" is a real question.

## 3. CONCRETE -- Make It Real

Memory is Velcro: the more hooks, the better it sticks. Concrete details are hooks. Abstractions are slippery.

Jerry Kaplan pitched Kleiner Perkins with no slides — a maroon leather portfolio tossed on the table: "Here's a model of the next step in the computer revolution." They couldn't stop touching it. They invested in a company that didn't exist yet.

"List white things" vs "list white things in your refrigerator" — people list almost as many for the fridge, because a real place gives the brain something to search.

James Grant (UNICEF) carried a packet of salt and sugar: this costs less than a cup of tea and can save hundreds of thousands of children's lives. A prop beats a scientific description.

Decide through a specific person. Saddleback Sam is the unchurched guy in their area, late thirties. Everyone in the org can ask what Sam would do. A Hamburger Helper manager remembered a woman with a toddler on her hip mixing dinner — that beat the data binders.

## 4. CREDIBLE -- Make It Believable

Let people test the idea themselves.

**Anti-authorities can beat experts.** Pam Laffin wasn't a doctor; she was a 29-year-old smoker dying of emphysema. Dennis the driver had been homeless; he was living proof.

**Vivid details make claims feel true**, even ones that shouldn't matter. "He uses a Star Wars toothbrush that looks like Darth Vader" moved a juror's rating of a mother (5.8 vs 4.3). Urban legends always nail a local detail.

**Shrink numbers to human scale.** Nuclear warheads as BBs. "One soft drink a month would double our aid to Africa."

**Sinatra Test:** one example so strong it proves the rest. "If I can make it there, I'll make it anywhere."

**Testable credentials:** Reagan's "ask yourself if you're better off than four years ago." Don't ask them to trust you — give them a check they can run.

Show the seventy-three-year-old dancing. The demo is the proof.

## 5. EMOTIONAL -- Make Them Care

Feel for a person, not a statistic. "If I look at the mass, I will never act. If I look at the one, I will act." (Mother Teresa.) Emotions don't scale.

**WIIFY** — what's in it for you — is the reliable trigger. Don't stop at base self-interest. Ask what kind of person they want to be.

"Don't mess with Texas" beat guilt and litter stats because it was about being Texan.

Skip worn words: unique, quality, excellence. Find fresh emotional territory.

## 6. STORIES -- Drive Action

Stories are flight simulators: they teach how to act and they make people want to. Firefighters swap stories after every fire for the same reason.

Three plots to look for, not invent:

- **Challenge:** beat the odds (Jared, 425 lbs to 180 on Subway; David vs. Goliath) — take the hard thing on
- **Connection:** bridge a gap between people (Good Samaritan) — help, tolerate, work together
- **Creativity:** see it a new way (Newton's apple) — try something

A springboard story is one concrete case that lets people see the larger pattern. Stephen Denning at the World Bank used a health worker in Zambia to show how knowledge sharing could change the place. Spot these in the wild; don't manufacture them. The Subway owner didn't invent Jared. He noticed, then amplified.

## The Villain: Curse of Knowledge

Elizabeth Newton's tappers/listeners (Stanford, 1990): tappers tap a well-known song; listeners guess. Tappers predict 50% will get it. Actual hit rate: 2.5% (3 of 120).

The tapper hears the song. The listener hears Morse code. Once you know, you can't un-know it.

SUCCESs is how you write for the listener.
